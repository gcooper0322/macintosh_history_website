The Macintosh PowerBook series was introduced in October 1991 as the successor to the Macintosh Portable. There were three initial models: the PowerBook 100 (low-end), 140 (mid-range), and 170 (high-end), all of which were packaged in notebook-style cases that essentially set the standard for laptop designs going forward. The PowerBook 100 used a 16MHz 68000 CPU; the 140 used a 16MHz 68030; and the 170 used a 25MHz 68030. The PowerBook 100 notably had a different case design from the other two; among other things, it was so thin that it had an external floppy drive instead of an internal one. These first three models sold for $2,500, $3,200, and $4,600 respectively.

The PowerBook 100 series was succeeded by several more, including the 500 series, 5300, 1400, 2400, and 3400. CPUs ranged from the 68030 and 68040 to the PowerPC 603 and 604 and beyond. The final few versions used PowerPC G3 and G4 CPUs before the PowerBook name was dropped with Apple's switch to Intel CPUs in 2006.
One notable sub-series, the PowerBook Duo series, was designed to be dual-purpose, being small and light (for the time) for portable use, and being able to slide into a special dock for desktop-like functionality when portability wasn't required. This series was sold from October 1992 to February 1997.
