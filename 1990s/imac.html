The iMac (retroactively the iMac G3) was introduced in May 1998 as Apple's consumer desktop offering. This machine was revolutionary for the time, packing a 233MHz PowerPC G3 CPU, 32MB of RAM, a 4GB hard drive, a CD-ROM drive, a 15-inch color CRT display, and all-new input/output standards such as USB into a sleek, curvy, eye-catching all-in-one desktop case made of translucent plastics in white and a deep turquoise referred to as "Bondi blue". It was intended as an approachable, easy-to-use computer for the Internet era (hence "iMac" being short for "Internet Mac"). Later revisions added more case color options - the first of these adding 5 new colors, all named after fruits such as "Strawberry" and "Lime" - with the model eventually having been produced in 13 different colors. The first variants sold for $1,299, lowered to $1,199 within the first year; later variants sold for as little as $999.
The iMac was an immediate, astronomical success, selling 278,000 units in its first six weeks and becoming the best-selling desktop computer in the United States for all of its first three months. Nearly half of all sales of the model were to first-time computer buyers, and nearly 20% were to Windows PC users who switched to the Mac. The iMac single-handedly propelled Apple back into profitability, with the company going from losing $878 million in 1997 to making $414 million in 1998. The model received refreshes in October 1999, July 2000, February 2001, and July 2001 that brought a revised design with a slot-loading CD or DVD drive, ever-faster hardware, and several new case colors. By the time it was succeeded by the iMac G4 in January 2002, it was said that 6 million iMac G3s had been sold.
